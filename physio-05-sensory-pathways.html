Sensory Pathways: how the body's signals find their way to the cortex.

Every touch, ache, and change in temperature travels a fixed three-neuron relay before it becomes a conscious sensation — but the two great ascending pathways don't take the same road, and they don't cross to the other side at the same place. That single difference is the key to localizing spinal cord injury at the bedside.

01 · Course orientation

What this lecture covers

A working map of the sensory (ascending) system: what carries what, where it crosses, and why that single fact is the most tested idea in the whole topic.

🎯 Learning objectives

  • Name the two major ascending pathways and explain why each carries an alternative name (anterolateral spinothalamic / dorsal column–medial lemniscal).
  • Trace the three-neuron relay (1st → 2nd → 3rd order) for each pathway, including where synapse and decussation occur.
  • List the sensory modalities and fiber types carried by each pathway, and relate fiber type to conduction speed and localization quality.
  • Predict the pattern of sensory loss (ipsilateral vs. contralateral) produced by a unilateral spinal cord lesion.
  • Explain the two-point discrimination test and the concept of a receptive field.
  • Describe the somatotopic organization (homunculus) of the primary somatosensory cortex (S1) and the role of S2.

📌 Key definitions

Ascending (sensory) pathway — A chain of neurons that carries information from peripheral sensory receptors to the brain so it can be discriminated/interpreted.
Decussation (crossing) — The point at which a pathway's fibers cross to the opposite side of the CNS. Every ascending pathway in this lecture eventually decussates — the difference is only where.
First / second / third order neuron — The fixed three-neuron sequence every sensation must pass through: periphery→cord (1st), cord/medulla→thalamus (2nd, crosses), thalamus→cortex (3rd).
Receptive field — The group of sensory receptors that converge onto, and are represented by, a single neuron.
02 · Neuroanatomy

Spinal cord cross-section: roots & sensory distribution

Before the pathways, the basic wiring rule: everything sensory comes in dorsally, everything motor goes out ventrally.

⇢ Root rule

All sensory fibers enter the spinal cord via the dorsal root. All motor fibers exit via the ventral root. This is true for both ascending pathways in this lecture — the first-order neuron always arrives dorsally.

📍 Regional distribution of sensation

DorsalFine touch, fine pressure, proprioception (position, movement, rate of movement)

AnteriorCrude touch, crude pressure, tickling, itching, sexual sensation

The same logic applies one level up — cranial nerves that are "mixed" carry an equivalent sensory ganglion and motor nucleus arrangement.

02 · Neuroanatomy

The three-neuron relay

Every stimulus, whichever pathway it takes, must be relayed through exactly three neurons, always in the same order, before it reaches conscious awareness.

This is the opening idea of the whole lecture: sensation entering on one side of the body ends up being processed on the opposite side of the brain. Left travels right, right travels left — because crossing (decussation) happens somewhere along the way, without exception.

1st order neuronEnters cord via dorsal root, straight from the receptor
→
2nd order neuronSynapses with 1st order · decussates · ascends to thalamus
→
3rd order neuronSynapses in thalamus · projects to somatosensory cortex

⭐⭐⭐ Must-know pearl

Wherever the crossing happens — spinal cord or medulla — it always happens. This is why a sensation arising on one side of the body is ultimately processed in the opposite cerebral hemisphere.

Where the two pathways actually differ

The 1st and 3rd order neurons behave the same way in both pathways. The only difference is the 2nd order neuron — specifically, where it synapses and where it crosses.

Spinothalamic tractDorsal column tract
1st order synapseDorsal horn (spinal cord)No synapse in cord — ascends ipsilaterally to medulla
2nd order synapseDorsal hornGracile / cuneate nuclei (medulla)
Crossing (decussation)Spinal cord, immediatelyMedulla
3rd order synapseThalamusThalamus (VPL nucleus)

Because these fibers are slow → sensations are poorly localized, slowly conducted, and less discriminated.

Sensations carried

PainTemperature (warm/cold)Crude touch & pressureItchTickleSexual sensation

"Crude" means non-specific and poorly localized — like feeling a cloth rather than being able to pinpoint a pin. Itch and tickle both arise from stimulation of a group of receptors together, which is why they feel related. The lateral column carries pain + temperature; the anterior column carries crude touch/pressure + itch/tickle + sexual sensation — together, "anterolateral."

🧭 Dorsal column–medial lemniscal (DCML) pathway crosses at medulla

Named "medial lemniscal" because the crossed second-order fibers ascend as the medial lemniscus.

Slow, thin, unmyelinated fibers (Type C, A-delta) carry crude, poorly-localized sensation via the spinothalamic tract. Fast, thick, myelinated fibers (A-beta, A-alpha) carry fine, precisely-localized sensation via the dorsal column.

03 · Neurophysiology

Somatosensory cortex — S1 & S2

The final stop for both pathways: where sensation becomes conscious, localized, and interpreted.

S1 — Primary somatosensory area

  • Located in the postcentral gyrus (recall: sensory = post-central, motor = pre-central).
  • Receives signal from the contralateral side of the body — because crossing has already occurred in both pathways, whether at the cord (spinothalamic) or the medulla (dorsal column).
  • Exception: the face is represented bilaterally in both hemispheres, since facial sensation arrives via the trigeminal nerve.

S2 — Secondary somatosensory area

  • Located below and behind the lower part of S1.
  • Receives input from S1, the visual area, the auditory area, and the thalamus.
  • Function: helps S1 with further discrimination — refining exactly what the sensation represents.

⭐⭐ Why the homunculus is upside-down — and why that's protective

Body representation in S1 is inverted: the area controlling the foot is mapped superiorly/medially, while the head is mapped inferiorly/laterally. The lecturer's clinical reasoning: this is protective by design — a blow to the top of the head is, by gravity and mechanics, more likely to affect the leg-representation area than the head/face area. Damage to the leg-control area is comparatively survivable and compatible with independent living; damage to the head/face area would be far more critical.

⭐⭐⭐ Must-know pearl

Cortical area size is proportional to receptor density, not physical body size. The face, lips, and fingers are densely packed with receptors and so occupy a disproportionately large area of S1; the trunk, with few receptors, occupies very little.

04 · Clinical examination

Clinical correlation & localization

Two bedside ideas turn this lecture from theory into something you can use on a ward round.

📏 Two-point discrimination test

Tests the integrity of the dorsal column pathway (specifically fine touch, carried by A-beta fibers). A pair of calipers touches the skin at two points simultaneously; the examiner finds the minimum distance between the points that is still perceived as two separate touches rather than one.

This works because of the receptive field concept: a group of receptors converging onto a single neuron. Any number of stimuli landing within one receptive field are felt as one sensation; stimuli landing in two different receptive fields are felt as two sensations.

🧊 Brown-Séquard localization — the "ice cube test"

A cold stimulus tests whether the spinothalamic tract is intact — because it should have already crossed at spinal cord level. Try the two scenarios below.

Scenario: a lesion on the left side of the spinal cord. Cold is applied to the right hand. Where should the patient feel it if the tract is intact?

🧭 Worked example: left-sided spinal cord lesion

PathwayWhere sensory loss appearsWhy
Spinothalamic (pain/temperature)Right sideAlready crossed at the cord — loss shows up contralateral to the lesion
Dorsal column (fine touch/vibration)Left side (same as lesion)Has not yet crossed at cord level — loss shows up ipsilateral to the lesion

🩺 Bedside takeaway

If cold applied to one hand is felt on the opposite side → spinothalamic tract intact, no damage. If it is not felt at all but touch is felt on the same side → suggests a spinothalamic lesion with an intact dorsal column — the classic Brown-Séquard (hemisection) pattern.

⭐⭐⭐ The one fact examiners love

Spinothalamic crosses at the spinal cord → contralateral loss. Dorsal column crosses at the medulla → ipsilateral loss. If you remember nothing else, remember this.

🧠 Mnemonics

"CITS" — anterior column contentsCrude touch, Itch, Tickle, Sexual sensation — the four things the anterior column carries alongside crude pressure.
"DCML crosses Downstairs... no wait, upstairs"Trick: Dorsal Column crosses in the Medulla — both start with a hard consonant sound near the top of the brainstem. Spinothalamic crosses at the Spinal cord — both start with "S," and it happens at the Start (entry level).
"Fine = Fast"Fine touch/vibration/proprioception → fast, myelinated A-alpha/A-beta fibers → precisely localized. Crude/pain/temp → slow A-delta/C fibers → poorly localized.
