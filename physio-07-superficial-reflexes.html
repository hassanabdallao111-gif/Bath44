Superficial Reflexes — Overview

Superficial reflexes are elicited by stimulating the skin or mucous membranes, in contrast to deep (muscle-stretch) reflexes which are elicited by tapping a tendon. Examining both deep and superficial reflexes lets you localize the level of a neurological lesion along the reflex arc.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define each of the four superficial reflexes and their clinical importance.
  • Trace the reflex arc for the withdrawal (flexor) reflex and the crossed extensor reflex.
  • Describe the correct technique, normal response, and abnormal response for the plantar, abdominal, and cremasteric reflexes.
  • Recall the spinal cord segmental level (center) of each reflex.
  • Differentiate physiological from pathological causes of an abnormal response for each reflex.
  • Master goal: use reflex findings to localize the level of a lesion in the nervous system.

🗂️ The Four Superficial Reflexes

1. Withdrawal (Flexor) Reflex
Painful stimulus → limb withdraws via flexor muscle contraction. Protective.
2. Crossed Extensor Reflex
Accompanies withdrawal — the opposite limb extends to bear the shifted body weight.
3. Plantar Reflex
Stroking the sole → normal toe flexion; abnormal = Babinski sign (dorsiflexion + fanning).
4. Abdominal Reflex
Scratching the abdominal skin toward the umbilicus → contraction of underlying abdominal wall muscle.
Bonus — Cremasteric Reflex: scratching the inner thigh in males → elevation of the testis via cremaster muscle contraction.

⭐ Why This Matters Clinically

The core exam objective behind testing deep and superficial reflexes is always the same: determine the localization and level of the lesion. Superficial reflexes test a polysynaptic spinal arc that runs through skin receptors → sensory neuron → interneuron → alpha motor neuron → muscle, so an abnormal response can point to a lesion anywhere along that arc, or reveal an upper motor neuron lesion releasing a primitive reflex (Babinski).

Superficial Reflex 1 of 4

Withdrawal Reflex ("Flexor Reflex")

A protective polysynaptic spinal reflex that pulls a limb away from a painful stimulus.

📖 Definition

Application of a painful stimulus to a limb leads to reflex contraction of the flexor muscles — i.e. withdrawal of the limb away from the painful stimulus.

Importance

Protective reflex — it prevents tissue destruction and removes the limb (or other body part) from the source of a painful stimulus.
Example the lecturer used: stepping on a nail → you instantly withdraw your foot before consciously "deciding" to.

  • Action potentials from painful stimuli are conducted by sensory (afferent) neurons to the spinal cord (the center).
  • The sensory neuron synapses with an excitatory interneuron — this makes it a polysynaptic reflex (unlike the monosynaptic deep tendon reflex).
  • The interneuron synapses with alpha motor neurons.
  • Alpha motor neurons stimulate the muscle that removes the limb from the painful stimulus — usually a flexor muscle, whether in the upper or lower limb.
Original schematic — withdrawal (flexor) reflex arc: nociceptor → sensory neuron → excitatory interneuron → alpha motor neuron → flexor muscle.

🚨 High-Yield Point ⭐⭐ High Yield

The withdrawal reflex is polysynaptic (sensory neuron → interneuron → motor neuron) unlike the monosynaptic deep tendon reflex. Its purpose is purely protective.

Superficial Reflex — Paired with Withdrawal

Crossed Extensor Reflex

Occurs simultaneously with the withdrawal reflex to keep you from falling when one limb is pulled away from a painful stimulus.

📖 Definition

Application of a painful stimulus to a limb leads to reflex withdrawal of the ipsilateral (stimulated) limb, while the opposite (contralateral) limb extends and body weight shifts onto it.
Classic example: When you step on a nail with your left foot, you withdraw your left foot (flexion) and, at the same time, shift your weight onto your right foot (extension) — you cannot lift both legs at once or you would fall.

⚡ Reflex Arc — Pathway

  • Sensory neurons activate interneurons in the spinal cord.
  • Interneurons stimulate alpha motor neurons → results in withdrawal (flexion) of the stimulated limb.
  • At the same time, collateral axons of these interneurons cross and activate alpha motor neurons innervating the extensor muscles on the opposite side of the body.
  • On the stimulated side: flexor muscles are stimulated (contract) while extensor muscles are inhibited.
  • On the opposite (unaffected) side: the pattern is reversed — extensor muscles are stimulated while flexor muscles are inhibited, so that limb can bear the shifted weight.
Original schematic — crossed extensor reflex: ipsilateral flexion + inhibited extension, paired with contralateral extension + inhibited flexion via crossing collateral axons.

🧠 Memory Trick

"Withdraw and stand tall" — the injured side bends (flexion) to escape pain, the healthy side straightens (extension) to keep you standing. One side always mirrors the other.
Superficial Reflex 3 of 4 · Extremely High-Yield

Plantar Reflex & the Babinski Sign

One of the most exam-famous signs in all of neurology ("ودي مشهورة شديد" — "and this one is very famous").

🩺 Technique

Stimulus: Draw a blunt object (e.g. a key) along the lateral side of the sole of the foot, moving from behind forward (from the heel toward the toes).
Original schematic — plantar reflex stimulus path and the normal (toe flexion) vs abnormal Babinski (big-toe dorsiflexion + fanning) response.
FeatureDetail
Center (spinal segments)L4 – S2
Normal responsePlantar flexion of all toes
Abnormal responseDorsiflexion of the big toe + fanning (spreading) of the other toes — the Babinski sign

Causes of an Abnormal (Babinski) Response ⭐⭐⭐ Must Know

✅ Physiological (normal in these states)

  • Deep sleep
  • General anesthesia
  • Infants below 1 year of age (corticospinal tract not yet myelinated / immature nervous system)

🚨 Pathological

  • Upper Motor Neuron Lesion (UMNL)
  • Deep coma
In children, a Babinski-type response is normal and not a cause for concern — it only becomes pathological once the corticospinal tract has fully myelinated (beyond infancy).
Superficial Reflex 4 of 4

Abdominal Reflexes

Superficial cutaneous reflex of the anterior abdominal wall, tested in four quadrants.

🩺 Technique

Stimulus: Gentle scratching of the skin of the abdomen using a pointed object, moving toward the midline (umbilicus) from the periphery.
Original schematic — abdominal reflex is tested in all four quadrants, scratching from lateral toward the umbilicus.
FeatureDetail
Center (spinal segments)T8 – T12
Normal responseContraction of the underlying muscles of the abdominal wall
Abnormal responseAbsence of contraction of the underlying abdominal wall muscle

Causes of Absent Abdominal Reflex

✅ Physiological

Weakness of the anterior abdominal wall muscles, as in:

  • Obese persons
  • Multiparous women (multiple pregnancies)

🚨 Pathological

  • Upper Motor Neuron Lesion (UMNL)
  • Lower Motor Neuron Lesion (LMNL) affecting the reflex arc itself
Clinical pearl: Do not immediately assume an absent abdominal reflex means a UMN or LMN lesion — always correlate with body habitus (obesity) and obstetric history (multiparity) first.

Exam Cram

High-Yield Exam Zone

The pearls most likely to appear on an exam, ranked by importance.

1. Exam Pearls

  • Reflex localization — the primary objective of testing deep and superficial reflexes is to determine the exact level of a lesion. ⭐⭐⭐
  • Babinski sign — extremely high-yield. Know both the normal response (plantar flexion of all toes) and the abnormal response (dorsiflexion of the big toe + fanning of the other toes). ⭐⭐⭐
  • Physiological Babinski: deep sleep, general anesthesia, infants < 1 year (immature myelination). ⭐⭐
  • Pathological Babinski: highly indicative of an Upper Motor Neuron (UMN) lesion or deep coma. ⭐⭐⭐
  • Reflex centers to memorize: Plantar = L4–S2 · Abdominal = T8–T12 · Cremasteric = L1–L2. ⭐⭐⭐
  • An absent abdominal reflex can be completely physiological in obese individuals or multiparous women — not automatically a neurological deficit. ⭐
  • Crossed extensor reflex is always tested conceptually alongside the withdrawal reflex — the stimulated limb flexes while the opposite limb extends to bear shifted body weight. ⭐⭐

2. Clinical Pearls

  • Plantar reflex technique: use a blunt object (like a key), draw along the lateral sole from behind (peripheral) forward toward the center.
  • Abdominal reflex technique: gentle scratching with a pointed object moving from the periphery strictly toward the midline (umbilicus).
  • Bedside caution: don't assume an absent abdominal reflex is an UMN/LMN lesion — always correlate with body habitus and obstetric history first.
  • Protective value: the primary functional goal of the withdrawal reflex is fundamentally protective — preventing tissue destruction.
Terminology

Key Vocabulary

Nociceptor — a sensory receptor in the skin that specifically detects painful (noxious) stimuli.
Polysynaptic reflex — a reflex arc that includes one or more interneurons between the sensory and motor neuron, so it crosses more than one synapse (as opposed to a monosynaptic deep tendon reflex).
Ipsilateral — on the same side of the body as the stimulus (the withdrawing limb).
Contralateral — on the opposite side of the body from the stimulus (the extending limb).
Dorsiflexion — upward bending of the big toe (or foot) toward the shin — the hallmark of an abnormal (Babinski) plantar response.
Fanning — spreading apart of the smaller toes, seen alongside big-toe dorsiflexion in the Babinski sign.
Upper Motor Neuron Lesion (UMNL) — damage to the corticospinal (pyramidal) pathway above the anterior horn cell; releases primitive reflexes such as the Babinski sign.
Lower Motor Neuron Lesion (LMNL) — damage to the reflex arc itself (anterior horn cell, root, nerve, or muscle), which abolishes reflexes rather than releasing them.
Multiparous — a woman who has given birth multiple times; associated with laxity of the anterior abdominal wall muscles.
