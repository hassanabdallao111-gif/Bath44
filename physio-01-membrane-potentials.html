Introduction to Neurophysiology & Membrane Potentials

A complete walkthrough of resting membrane potential, the action potential, synaptic transmission, and the pharmacology that depends on it all — the electrical language every neuron and muscle fiber speaks — built for rapid understanding and exam recall.

01

Overview & Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture you should be able to describe how the nervous system detects and responds to stimuli, explain the ionic basis of the resting membrane potential, and walk through every stage of the action potential in a sensory neuron.

What this lecture covers

  • The three broad functions of the nervous system
  • Neurons vs. glial (supporting) cells, and the role of each glial cell type
  • The definition and measurement of membrane potential
  • The resting membrane potential (RMP) and its three ionic bases
  • The stages and ionic basis of the action potential (AP)
  • Voltage-gated Na⁺ channel gating and the role of Ca²⁺
  • The three gating classes of membrane channels, and how channels differ from pumps
  • Synaptic transmission, EPSPs/IPSPs, and temporal vs. spatial summation
  • The neuromuscular junction and its major clinical disorders
  • How local anesthetics and anti-epileptics exploit Na⁺ channel gating
  • Absolute and relative refractory periods
  • Conduction velocity and the organization of the nervous system
  • Factors that raise or lower nerve/muscle excitability
Why it matters

The action potential is the "only language" the nervous system speaks — every reflex, sensation, thought, and movement ultimately depends on this single electrical event. Understanding how the Na⁺ channel gates work is the key that later unlocks local anesthesia, anti-epileptic drugs, and anticonvulsant pharmacology.

02

Functions of the Nervous System

Every function of the nervous system falls into one of three categories.

👀

1. Sensory functions

Detect changes in the internal or external environment — these changes are called stimuli. A stimulus is any change detectable by the body. Internal examples include the chemoreceptor reflex (detecting O₂, CO₂, acidosis, alkalosis) and the baroreceptor reflex (detecting changes in blood pressure) — both exist to preserve homeostasis.

🏃

2. Motor functions

Give the appropriate response to a stimulus. In involuntary activity, sensory always precedes motor — you see a threatening sight (sensory) then flee (motor); you smell something unpleasant (sensory) then move away (motor). Motor activity can also be voluntary, achieved with no external stimulus at all — e.g. deciding, unprompted, to walk to the cafeteria.

🧠

3. Higher intellectual functions

Functions related to intelligence: memory, learning, and mental activity (cognition). This is the function that most distinguishes humans from other animals — animals share sensory function, motor function, and reflexes with us, but higher intellectual function is far more developed in humans.

03

Neurons & Glial (Supporting) Cells

The CNS and PNS are built from two categories of cells.

Neurons ~10%

  • The main functional cells of the nervous system
  • Excitable cells — capable of generating an action potential
  • The action potential spreads/propagates along the nerve as a nerve impulse — the only "language" the nervous system uses

Supporting (glial) cells ~90%

  • Also called sustentacular cells; "glial" is Latin for support
  • General classification: microglia vs. macroglia
  • Macroglia = astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, Schwann cells (PNS); some texts add ependymal cells (lining cells)
⭐

Astrocytes Most abundant glial cell

Star-shaped ("astro" = star-like). Seven key functions:

  1. Hold neurons together in proper spatial orientation, like scaffolding that keeps neurons from becoming disorganized in space
  2. Act as a scaffold that guides migrating neurons to their correct final destination during fetal brain development
  3. Uptake and degrade released neurotransmitters (NTs) — NT action must be terminated, either by uptake (into another cell) or reuptake (back into the same releasing neuron, e.g. acetylcholine), or by enzymatic metabolism
  4. Induce brain capillaries to form the blood–brain barrier (BBB) by wrapping processes around capillaries, restricting direct contact between blood and the interstitial fluid/CSF, and creating a selective barrier
  5. Help transfer nutrients from blood to neurons via their close association with capillaries
  6. Take up excess K⁺ to keep brain-ECF/CSF K⁺ at a normal, low level, protecting neural excitability
  7. Enhance synapse formation and strengthen synaptic transmission via chemical signaling
Clinical correlation

Only two systems in the body are protected by a dedicated barrier: the blood–brain barrier and the blood–testis barrier — the two most "sensitive" systems (nervous and reproductive). This is why inflammation is tolerated differently by different organs: enteritis or gastritis are considered ordinary, but encephalitis or orchitis are treated as serious.

K⁺ buffering — the numbers

Plasma K⁺ ≈ 3.5–5.5 mEq/L (average ~4 mEq/L). Astrocytes keep CSF/brain-ECF K⁺ lower, around 2.5 mEq/L, because K⁺ strongly affects neuronal excitability.

🧡

Oligodendrocytes

Form the myelin sheath around axons of central neurons (brain and spinal cord).

Myelination increases the conduction velocity of the action potential. If a myelinated nerve undergoes demyelination, its conduction becomes slower than that of an originally non-myelinated fiber.

🛡️

Microglia

The brain macrophages (CNS macrophages) — act as phagocytic scavenger cells, analogous to Kupffer cells in the liver or pulmonary macrophages in the lung. They also release nerve growth factor.

💧

Ependymal cells

Lining cells of the internal cavities of the brain and spinal cord. Most CSF is produced at the choroid plexus, which is lined by ependymal cells, so they contribute directly to CSF formation. They can also serve as neural stem cells with the potential to form new neurons and glial cells.

🦢

Schwann cells

Form the myelin sheath around peripheral neurons.

Clinical correlation — diabetic neuropathy

Diabetes causes peripheral neuropathy, not central neuropathy. Chronic hyperglycemia produces non-enzymatic glycosylation of proteins, which damages Schwann cells but spares oligodendrocytes. The resulting demyelination slows nerve impulse conduction, producing the sensory weakness of peripheral neuropathy.

04

Membrane Potential — The Basics

Membrane potential is the difference in electrical potential between the inside and outside of the cell membrane — the very same "electricity" you already know, just measured in millivolts inside the body.

Three names for the same thing

Potential / Electrical potential Electromotive force (EMF) Voltage

All three terms describe the same electrical quantity, measured in millivolts (mV) in physiology. This is why a channel that opens in response to a change in this quantity is called a voltage-gated channel.

Types of membrane potential

Resting membrane potential (RMP), action potential (AP), excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP), inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP), and receptor potential are all examples of membrane potentials.

⭐ High yield — definition

Resting Membrane Potential (RMP)

A state of polarization in which the inside of the cell membrane is negative relative to the outside.

TissueRMP
Central / sensory neurons−70 mV
Skeletal muscle−90 mV
Concept check — negativity is relative

The negativity of RMP is relative, not absolute — electrical potential in general has no absolute negative value (which is exactly why it's measured in millivolts rather than as a "negative quantity" on its own). Example: if the inside potential is 70 (in magnitude) and the outside is 140, then relative to outside, inside = 70 − 140 = −70 mV, while outside relative to inside = 140 − 70 = +70 mV. Outside is relatively positive; inside is relatively negative — but both are simply magnitudes of the same electrical quantity, compared to each other.

05

Genesis of the RMP — Three Ionic Bases

The inside of the membrane is negative relative to the outside for three reasons.

1. Potassium leakage the first & most important event

At rest, K⁺ leaks out of the cell more readily than Na⁺ leaks in. This net efflux of a cation leaves the inside relatively negative.

Why does K⁺ leak more than Na⁺? Compare their atomic structure: Na⁺ has electron configuration 2, 8, 1 (three shells); K⁺ has 2, 8, 8, 1 (four shells). K⁺'s outermost electron sits farther from the nucleus than Na⁺'s. Because Na⁺'s outer orbit is closer to its nucleus, Na⁺ attracts surrounding water molecules more strongly — so the hydrated Na⁺ ion is actually larger than the hydrated K⁺ ion (K⁺ cannot pull in as much water). The smaller hydrated K⁺ passes through leak channels more easily than the larger hydrated Na⁺, so K⁺ leaks out preferentially.

2. The Na⁺–K⁺ pump electrogenic pump

Each pump cycle moves 3 Na⁺ ions outside and 2 K⁺ ions inside — a net loss of one positive charge from inside the cell with every cycle. This is why the Na⁺–K⁺ pump is described as electrogenic: it directly generates electrical potential by making the inside more negative.

3. Non-diffusible intracellular anions

Large intracellular anions — proteins, phosphates, and sulfates — cannot easily diffuse across the membrane. They remain trapped inside the cell and contribute directly to its internal negativity.

Mnemonic
"Leak, Lose, Linger" — K⁺ Leaks out, the pump makes you Lose a charge every cycle, and anions Linger inside because they can't diffuse out.
06

Neuron Structure — A Quick Recap

Before covering the action potential, recall the basic parts of a neuron and where each electrical event happens.

  • Cell body (soma) — contains the nucleus; responsible for synthesis and release of neurotransmitters that are peptides/proteins (any protein/peptide NT must be synthesized here, where the DNA is)
  • Dendrites — small processes; the site of generation of local potentials — a receptor potential in a sensory neuron, or an EPSP/IPSP in a motor neuron. Local potentials only trigger an action potential once they undergo summation
  • Axon — begins at the axon hillock; myelination (by oligodendrocytes centrally or Schwann cells peripherally) always occurs along the axon
  • Axon terminal — branches into terminal buttons (synaptic knobs), the site of neurotransmitter storage and release at the synapse
07

The Action Potential (AP) & Excitability

⭐ High yield — definitions

Action potential: a sudden reversal of the RMP — the inside becomes positive relative to the outside.

Excitability: the ability of a tissue to generate an action potential in response to a stimulus. Excitable tissues = nerve and muscle.

Excitability in everyday terms

Excitability is what lets a receptor convert a stimulus into an action potential: rods and cones in the retina convert light into an AP (vision); the cochlea converts sound into an AP (hearing); sensory neurons convert touch or pain into an AP (somatic sensation).

Stages of AP genesis in a sensory neuron

Stage 1 Polarization

This is simply the resting membrane potential stage — the inside is relatively negative, at −70 mV.

Stage 2 Depolarization

The stimulus opens stimulus-sensitive, non-specific cation channels. The name describes exactly what they are:

  • Cation channels — they let positive ions through
  • Non-specific — Na⁺, Ca²⁺, or Mg²⁺ can pass, depending on the neuron
  • Stimulus-sensitive — they open in response to the stimulus

Cation influx moves the membrane potential from −70 mV toward the threshold potential of −55 mV (threshold = "the doorway" — the point at which the door opens and the cell commits to firing). At threshold, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open (named for exactly this — they open due to a change in voltage, from closed at −70 mV to open at −55 mV). Na⁺ enters faster than K⁺ can leave, producing rapid depolarization that overshoots past zero to about +35 mV in neurons (up to +55 mV in some tissues). The Na⁺ channels then close and cannot reopen until the cell returns toward the resting state.

Why this matters clinically

Understanding how these Na⁺ channel gates behave is essential groundwork for understanding local anesthesia, anti-epileptic drugs, and anticonvulsant pharmacology — all of which act by modifying this gating behavior.

Stage 3 Repolarization

Caused by opening of voltage-gated K⁺ channels. Since K⁺ is concentrated intracellularly and Na⁺ extracellularly, this stage is characterized by K⁺ efflux (as Na⁺ influx occurs in the previous stage). The membrane returns toward negative, but the cell has not yet returned to its true resting state — Na⁺ is still elevated inside and K⁺ is still low inside; only the electrical potential has recovered, not the normal ionic composition.

Stage 4 Restoration by the Na⁺–K⁺ pump

The Na⁺–K⁺ pump returns Na⁺ to the outside and K⁺ to the inside, restoring the normal chemical (ionic) composition on each side of the membrane — not just the electrical potential. This is one of the pump's most important roles: maintaining normal Na⁺/K⁺ distribution between intracellular and extracellular fluid.

Mnemonic
"Please Don't Rest" — Polarization → Depolarization → Repolarization → pump Restores.

Duration of the action potential

The AP lasts 2–4 milliseconds in nerve and skeletal muscle.

Voltage-Gated Na⁺ Channels

These are protein channels that respond to changes in the electrical potential difference across the membrane. Each channel has two gates:

  • Activation gate — the outer gate
  • Inactivation gate — the inner gate
Gate stateActivation (outer) gateInactivation (inner) gate
RestingClosedOpen
Depolarization (activated)OpenOpen
InactivatedOpenClosed
⭐ High yield

Extracellular Ca²⁺ acts as a guard for these channels — an increase in extracellular Ca²⁺ decreases Na⁺ influx and therefore decreases excitability (and vice versa). This is the mechanistic link behind why hypocalcemia raises excitability and hypercalcemia lowers it — see the excitability table later in this lecture.

09

Membrane Channels & Pumps — The Full Picture

Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are only one member of a much larger family. Every channel is classified by what opens it and what it lets through.

⚡

Voltage-gated

Open in response to a change in membrane potential. Examples: the Na⁺ and K⁺ channels of the action potential, and Ca²⁺ channels at the presynaptic terminal.

📡

Ligand (chemically) – gated

Open when a chemical messenger — a neurotransmitter, hormone, or ion — binds a receptor site on the channel. Example: the nicotinic ACh receptor at the neuromuscular junction.

👀

Mechanically-gated

Open in response to physical deformation of the membrane — stretch, pressure, or vibration. Examples: cochlear hair cell channels (hearing) and baroreceptor channels (blood pressure sensing).

Channel vs. pump — don't confuse them

ChannelPump (e.g. Na⁺–K⁺ ATPase)
MovementPassive, down the electrochemical gradientActive, against the gradient
EnergyNone requiredRequires ATP
SpeedExtremely fast (~milliseconds)Much slower, continuous cycling
Role in APGenerates the rapid electrical eventRestores ionic composition afterward

Synaptic Transmission & Local Potentials

A chemical synapse converts an electrical signal into a chemical one, then back into an electrical one — this is how the nerve impulse crosses the gap between two cells.

🔮 Sequence of events
AP reaches terminal
→
Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open
→
Ca²⁺ influx
→
Vesicles fuse & release NT
→
NT diffuses & binds receptor
→
Postsynaptic channels open

The result is a local (graded) potential in the postsynaptic membrane — not yet an action potential.

EPSP excitatory

An excitatory postsynaptic potential is a depolarizing local potential, usually produced by Na⁺ influx through ligand-gated channels. It pushes the membrane toward threshold.

IPSP inhibitory

An inhibitory postsynaptic potential is a hyperpolarizing (or stabilizing) local potential, usually produced by Cl⁻ influx or K⁺ efflux. It pushes the membrane away from threshold.

⭐ High yield — graded vs. action potential
PropertyLocal (graded) potentialAction potential
AmplitudeVariable, proportional to stimulusFixed, all-or-none
SpreadDecremental (dies out with distance)Non-decremental (self-regenerating)
SummationCan summate (temporal & spatial)Cannot summate
Refractory periodNonePresent (absolute & relative)
Channels involvedLigand- or mechanically-gatedVoltage-gated

Summation — how local potentials add up

  • Temporal summation: the same presynaptic terminal fires repeatedly, in rapid succession, before the previous local potential decays — the effects add up over time.
  • Spatial summation: several different presynaptic terminals fire together at the same moment, and their local potentials add up over space on the postsynaptic membrane.

Only once the summed local potential reaches threshold (−55 mV) at the axon hillock does a true action potential fire — this is why the dendrites/soma are described as an integrating zone.

Mnemonic
"E before I, E goes high" — EPSP depolarizes (goes up toward threshold); IPSP moves away (inhibits).
11

The Neuromuscular Junction (NMJ)

The NMJ is a specialized chemical synapse between a motor neuron's axon terminal and a skeletal muscle fiber — essentially a dedicated, highly reliable version of the general synapse just described.

How it works

The motor neuron releases acetylcholine (ACh), which binds nicotinic ACh receptors on the muscle's motor end plate. These receptors are themselves ligand-gated, non-specific cation channels — binding ACh opens them directly, allowing Na⁺ influx (and some K⁺ efflux).

This produces an end-plate potential (EPP), a local potential that (unlike most EPSPs) is normally large enough on its own to reach threshold and trigger a full muscle action potential, which then propagates along the sarcolemma and initiates contraction.

Clinical correlations

Myasthenia gravis: autoantibodies destroy nicotinic ACh receptors, weakening the EPP and causing fatigable muscle weakness. Botulinum toxin: blocks ACh vesicle release, causing flaccid paralysis. Organophosphates/nerve agents: inhibit acetylcholinesterase, so ACh lingers in the cleft and overstimulates the receptor, eventually causing paralysis from prolonged depolarization.

12

Na⁺ Channel Pharmacology — Why This All Matters

Everything covered about voltage-gated Na⁺ channel gating becomes directly useful here: several major drug classes work by manipulating exactly these gates.

Local anesthetics

Drugs like lidocaine enter the neuron and bind the inner pore of the voltage-gated Na⁺ channel, stabilizing the inactivated state. Na⁺ can no longer flow, so no depolarization — and no action potential — can occur in that segment of nerve. Small, unmyelinated pain and temperature fibers are blocked first, which is why pain sensation is lost before touch.

Anti-epileptic / anticonvulsant drugs

Drugs such as phenytoin and carbamazepine also stabilize the inactivated state of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels, but selectively target neurons firing abnormally fast (as in a seizure focus), since only rapidly-cycling channels spend enough time in the drug-preferred inactivated state to be affected.

Concept link

Both drug classes exploit the same two-gate model from the Voltage-Gated Na⁺ Channels section: activation (outer) gate and inactivation (inner) gate. Trap the inactivation gate shut, and the neuron cannot fire — regardless of how strong the stimulus is.

13

Refractory Periods

The refractory period (RP) is the period during which a nerve or muscle resists (is "refractory" to) re-excitation.

Absolute Refractory Period (ARP)

= Depolarization + first 1/3 of repolarization.

During the ARP, no stimulus — however strong — can re-excite the nerve. Na⁺ channels are already maximally open during depolarization, then enter their inactivated state (closed from inside, at the inactivation gate) during the first third of repolarization.

Relative Refractory Period (RRP)

= Remaining 2/3 of repolarization.

During the RRP, a stronger-than-normal stimulus can re-excite the neuron, because some Na⁺ channels have already returned to the activation-ready state (outer/activation gate closed, inner/inactivation gate open).

14

Conduction Velocity

  • Conduction velocity of the AP is faster in large-diameter nerve fibers than in small ones
  • Conduction is faster in myelinated fibers (types A and B) than in non-myelinated fibers (type C)
  • Neurons are classified functionally as afferent (carrying signals toward the CNS) or efferent (carrying signals away from the CNS)

Glossary

Stimulus
Any change, internal or external, that is detectable by the body.
Excitability
The ability of a tissue to generate an action potential in response to a stimulus.
Nerve impulse
An action potential that has propagated (spread) along a nerve.
Threshold potential
The membrane potential (−55 mV) at which voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open and an action potential becomes inevitable.
Overshoot
The portion of depolarization that carries the membrane potential past 0 mV to a positive value (~+35 mV).
Electrogenic pump
A pump that directly generates an electrical potential by producing a net charge movement — the Na⁺–K⁺ pump moves 3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ in.
Reuptake vs. uptake
Reuptake: neurotransmitter reabsorbed by the same neuron that released it. Uptake: absorbed by a different cell.
Afferent / Efferent
Afferent neurons carry impulses toward the CNS; efferent neurons carry impulses away from the CNS.
EPSP / IPSP
Excitatory/inhibitory postsynaptic potential — local, graded depolarizing or hyperpolarizing responses at a synapse.
Temporal summation
Repeated firing of one presynaptic terminal in rapid succession, adding up local potentials over time.
Spatial summation
Simultaneous firing of multiple presynaptic terminals, adding up local potentials across the membrane.
End-plate potential (EPP)
The local potential generated at the neuromuscular junction motor end plate; normally large enough to trigger a muscle action potential on its own.
Ligand-gated channel
A channel that opens when a chemical (neurotransmitter, hormone, ion) binds a receptor site on it, e.g. the nicotinic ACh receptor.

High-Yield Exam Zone

⭐⭐⭐ Must know
  • RMP = −70 mV (neurons) / −90 mV (skeletal muscle); it is a relative negativity
  • Three ionic bases of RMP: K⁺ leakage, electrogenic Na⁺–K⁺ pump (3 out / 2 in), non-diffusible intracellular anions
  • Threshold potential = −55 mV; AP overshoot ≈ +35 mV
  • Depolarization = Na⁺ influx; Repolarization = K⁺ efflux; restoration = Na⁺–K⁺ pump
  • AP duration = 2–4 ms
  • ARP = depolarization + first 1/3 repolarization (absolutely no re-excitation); RRP = remaining 2/3 (stronger stimulus can re-excite)
  • ↑ extracellular Ca²⁺ → ↓ Na⁺ influx → ↓ excitability (guard function)
  • Myelination increases conduction velocity; large fibers conduct faster than small ones
  • Astrocytes form the BBB, buffer K⁺, and guide neuron migration; oligodendrocytes myelinate CNS axons; Schwann cells myelinate PNS axons; diabetic neuropathy is peripheral (Schwann cell damage), not central

Spotter / Viva-style prompts

  1. A student says "the inside of a resting neuron is absolutely negative." Correct this statement.
  2. Explain, at the atomic level, why potassium leaks out of the cell more readily than sodium leaks in.
  3. Why is the Na⁺–K⁺ pump called "electrogenic"?
  4. A patient with hypocalcemia presents with increased neuromuscular excitability. Explain the ionic mechanism.
  5. Why does diabetes cause peripheral, but not central, neuropathy?
  6. During the absolute refractory period, why can no stimulus — regardless of strength — trigger a new action potential?
