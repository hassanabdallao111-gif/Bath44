The Reflex Arc & Stretch Reflex

A complete, exam-focused walkthrough of the reflex arc, the muscle spindle, dynamic and static stretch reflexes, muscle tone, the four major deep tendon reflexes, and the Golgi tendon (inverse stretch) reflex — with original diagrams, mnemonics, a mind map, flashcards, and an MCQ bank.

01CNS Overview & Learning Objectives

Where this lecture sits in the motor physiology sequence

This is the first lecture of the Motor section of the neuroscience block, following directly on from the sensory pathways and receptor physiology already covered. Every reflex depends on the receptor and pathway concepts learned earlier — this lecture builds directly on them.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture you should be able to:

  • List and explain the five components of the reflex arc
  • Classify reflexes by centre, synapse number, and receptor site
  • Describe the structure of the muscle spindle and its two fibre types
  • Differentiate dynamic vs static stretch reflex
  • Explain the clinical basis of muscle tone and the four deep tendon reflexes
  • Describe the Golgi tendon (inverse stretch) reflex and its protective role

⭐ Why this lecture matters clinically

The stretch reflex and deep tendon reflexes are the physiological basis of the motor examination — the single most reliable bedside tool for localizing lesions along the neuraxis (UMN vs LMN, spinal level, and even cerebellar/basal ganglia disease).

04The Stretch Reflex⭐⭐⭐ Must Know

The only true monosynaptic reflex in the human body

Definition

Stretch of a skeletal muscle leads to reflex contraction of the same muscle.

It is considered a feedback / protective mechanism that maintains the length of the muscle constant — stretch = lengthening, contraction = shortening. By contracting in response to stretch, the muscle is protected from rupture and returns toward its resting length.

Pathway (Reflex Arc) of the Stretch Reflex

ComponentStructure
StimulusStretch of the muscle
ReceptorMuscle spindle (intrafusal fibres — nuclear bag & nuclear chain)
AfferentPrimary & secondary endings
CentreAnterior Horn Cell (A.H.C.) of the spinal cord
EfferentAlpha motor neuron → extrafusal muscle fibres
ResponseMuscle contraction

Why it's monosynaptic

The sensory neuron from the muscle spindle synapses directly onto the alpha motor neuron in the anterior horn — one synapse, no interneuron. This is unique among all reflexes in the body and is why the stretch reflex is the fastest reflex response.

Clinical basis: All four tendon jerks tested in the motor exam (biceps, triceps, knee, ankle) are stretch reflexes elicited by tapping a tendon.

05The Muscle Spindle⭐⭐⭐ Must Know

Structure of the stretch reflex receptor

The muscle spindle contains specialized intrafusal muscle fibres, arranged parallel to and lying inside the contractile extrafusal muscle fibres (the "ordinary" skeletal muscle fibres that shorten the whole muscle).

Central part — non-contractile

This is the receptor site. It cannot contract, but it can be stretched.

Peripheral part — contractile

When the peripheral part contracts, it pulls outward — which stretches the central (receptor) part. This is the opposite of the whole muscle, which shortens toward its centre when it contracts.

The Two Types of Intrafusal Fibres

Nuclear Bag FibreNuclear Chain Fibre
Naming basisLarge number of nuclei concentrated in the central (receptor) partNuclei spread in a chain throughout the receptor area
Response speedRapidly responding & rapidly adaptingSlowly responding & slowly adapting
MediatesDynamic stretch reflex → tendon jerkStatic stretch reflex → muscle tone

How the Spindle Is Stimulated

1. Whole muscle stretched

e.g. tapping the muscle tendon with a reflex hammer → dynamic response.

2. Gamma discharge

When the peripheral (contractile) part of the intrafusal fibre contracts under gamma motor neuron drive, the central receptor area is stretched — even without any change in whole-muscle length.

06Afferent & Efferent Innervation⭐⭐ High Yield

Afferent Innervation (Sensory)

a. Primary Endings — Type Ia

  • Spiral around the central receptor area of the intrafusal fibre
  • Supply both nuclear bag and nuclear chain fibres
  • Discharge mainly while stretch is occurring
  • Afferent for the dynamic stretch reflex

b. Secondary Endings — Type II

  • Supply only the nuclear chain fibres
  • Discharge with maintained stretch
  • Responsible for the static stretch reflex

Both primary and secondary endings are stimulated whenever the central receptor area of the muscle spindle is stretched.

Efferent Innervation (Motor)

Gamma (γ) motor neurons

Supply the nuclear bag and nuclear chain fibres. Two subtypes: dynamic Aγ and static Aγ. Their discharge contracts the intrafusal fibres → stretches the non-contractile centre → stimulates primary and secondary endings.

Alpha (α) motor neurons

Innervate and contract the extrafusal muscle fibres — this produces the actual, visible muscle contraction (the reflex response).

Don't confuse: Gamma → intrafusal fibres (adjusts spindle sensitivity). Alpha → extrafusal fibres (produces the movement/response).

07Dynamic vs Static Stretch Reflex⭐⭐⭐ Must Know

Dynamic Stretch ReflexStatic Stretch Reflex
StimulusSudden stretch of the muscleMaintained stretch of the skeletal muscle
ReceptorNuclear bag fibresNuclear chain fibres
AfferentPrimary ending only (rapidly conducting)Mainly secondary ending (also primary)
EffectSudden contraction followed by rapid relaxationMaintained partial contraction of the muscle
FunctionBase of the tendon jerkBase of muscle tone

Action potential during stretch

Firing frequency of the spindle afferent increases during stretch and declines during contraction of the whole muscle — this is how the CNS continuously senses muscle length.

Memory hook

Dynamic = sudden = jerk. Static = maintained = tone.

08Supraspinal Centres Affecting the Stretch Reflex⭐⭐ High Yield

Higher centres modulate the excitability of the stretch reflex arc, either facilitating or inhibiting it.

Facilitatory centres

  • Primary motor area (area 4)
  • Neo-cerebellum
  • Facilitatory reticular formation
  • Vestibular nucleus

Damage to any of these → hyporeflexia (loss of facilitation).

Inhibitory centres

  • Premotor area (area 6)
  • Paleo-cerebellum
  • Inhibitory reticular formation
  • Red nucleus & basal ganglia

Damage to any of these → hyperreflexia (loss of inhibition).

09Muscle Tone — Clinical Significance⭐⭐⭐ Must Know

Definition

A mild, maintained contraction of skeletal muscle during rest, resisting stretch — the physiological expression of the static stretch reflex.

Distribution

Present in all muscles of the body, but most prominent in anti-gravity muscles: extensors of the lower limb & trunk, neck extensors, flexors of the upper limb, and elevators of the jaw.

Mechanism

The resting length of a muscle is normally shorter than the distance between its origin and insertion — so at rest, the muscle is always in a state of mild stretch. This continuous mild stretch continuously drives the static stretch reflex → continuous mild contraction = tone.

Abnormalities of Muscle Tone

Increased (Hypertonia)

CauseSign
Upper motor neuron lesionClasp-knife spasticity — high initial resistance then sudden relaxation
ParkinsonismLead-pipe rigidity — constant resistance throughout the whole range of movement

Decreased (Hypotonia)

Cause
Lower motor neuron lesion
Neo-cerebellar syndrome

Limb feels flaccid — falls limply when lifted and released.

Functions of Muscle Tone

1. Posture

Maintains body posture against gravity.

2. Venous return

Squeezes veins passing through skeletal muscle, aiding venous return.

3. Thermoregulation

Increases heat production during cold exposure.

4. Visceral support

Anterior abdominal wall tone keeps the viscera in position.

10Deep Tendon Reflexes (Tendon Jerks)⭐⭐⭐ Must Know

Tendon jerks are the dynamic stretch reflex in clinical action: a sudden stretch, produced by tapping the tendon, stimulates the muscle spindle, increases discharge from the primary ending, and produces a rapid contraction followed by rapid relaxation. These four reflexes form the foundation of the clinical motor examination.

1. Triceps Reflex

Stimulus: Tapping the triceps tendon.
Response: Contraction of triceps → extension of the elbow.
Centre: C6 & C7.

2. Biceps Reflex

Stimulus: Tapping the biceps tendon.
Response: Contraction of biceps → flexion of the elbow.
Centre: C5 & C6.

3. Knee Jerk (Patellar Reflex)

Stimulus: Tapping the patellar tendon.
Response: Contraction of quadriceps → extension of the knee.
Centre: L2, L3 & L4.

4. Ankle Jerk (Achilles Reflex)

Stimulus: Tapping the Achilles tendon.
Response: Contraction of gastrocnemius → plantar flexion of the foot.
Centre: S1 & S2.

Clinical Significance of DTRs

A normal reflex response indicates an intact reflex arc at that spinal level.

Exaggerated (Hyperreflexia)

  • Upper motor neuron lesion
  • Hyperthyroidism
  • Nervousness / anxiety

Decreased / Absent (Hyporeflexia)

  • Lower motor neuron lesion
  • Hypothyroidism
  • Sleep (physiological)
Clonus: a series of rhythmic, oscillating stretch reflexes elicited by a sudden sustained stretch (e.g. rapid dorsiflexion of the ankle). It is a definitive sign of an upper motor neuron lesion.

11Golgi Tendon Reflex (Inverse Stretch Reflex)⭐⭐ High Yield

Principle: Severe contraction of a muscle leads to relaxation of that same muscle — the opposite response to the stretch reflex, hence "inverse stretch reflex."

ComponentDetail
StimulusMarked muscle contraction (severe stretch of the tendon)
ReceptorGolgi tendon organ (tension receptor) — lies in the tendon, in series with extrafusal fibres
AfferentType Ib fibres
SynapsesBisynaptic — via an inhibitory interneuron
NeurotransmitterGlycine (inhibitory)
EffectRelaxation of the same muscle

Mechanism

Type Ib fibres synapse on an inhibitory interneuron, which releases glycine, causing hyperpolarization of the anterior horn cell of that muscle — inhibiting the alpha motor neuron and producing relaxation.

Original lecture image
Location of the Golgi tendon organ at the musculotendinous junction, in series with the extrafusal fibres (contrast with the muscle spindle, which lies in parallel).
Original schematic: severe tension detected by the Golgi tendon organ excites a Ib afferent, which drives an inhibitory (glycinergic) interneuron that hyperpolarizes the alpha motor neuron — relaxing the same muscle.
Original lecture image
Full circuit diagram: the Ib afferent from the Golgi tendon organ excites an inhibitory interneuron, which releases glycine onto the motor neuron supplying the same (flexor) muscle, while descending pathways can also modulate this interneuron.
Original lecture image
Step 1 — Muscle contraction (e.g. lifting a heavy load) stretches the Golgi tendon organ, which signals the spinal cord via the inhibiting interneuron.
Original lecture image
Steps 2 to 4 — The motor neuron is inhibited, the muscle relaxes, and the load is released: the protective sequence of the Golgi tendon (inverse stretch) reflex.

Function

A protective reflex that prevents rupture of the muscle or avulsion/separation of the tendon when tension becomes excessive (e.g. lifting an excessively heavy load).

Contrast with stretch reflex: Stretch reflex = monosynaptic, excitatory, protects against over-lengthening. Golgi tendon reflex = bisynaptic, inhibitory, protects against over-tension.

12Memory & Mnemonics

🔤
Reflex arc order — "R.A.C.E.R": Receptor → Afferent → Centre → Efferent → Response (Effector executes it).
🔤
Dynamic vs Static: "Dynamic = Deep tendon jerk (sudden)." "Static = Sustained tone."
🔤
Reflex centre levels (top to bottom): "S1-2 ankles the ground, L234 kicks the door, C56 flexes to see, C67 extends to push." — Ankle S1,S2 · Knee L2,3,4 · Biceps C5,6 · Triceps C6,7.
🔤
"Rule of 1s and 2s for extensors, mix for flexors": Triceps & Ankle are pure extensor pairs that step UP by one root (C6-7, S1-2); Biceps & Knee are flexor/extensor pairs that step DOWN (C5-6, L2-3-4).
🔤
Golgi = "GO relax": Golgi tendon Organ → inhibits → relaxation (opposite of stretch reflex).
🔤
Bag vs Chain: "Big BAG, quick reflex, BANGS the jerk (dynamic)." "long CHAIN, holds on, sustains the TONE (static)."

16Rapid Revision — Exam-Cram Sheet

Core Facts

  • Reflex arc = 5 components: Receptor–Afferent–Centre–Efferent–Effector
  • Stretch reflex = ONLY monosynaptic reflex in the body
  • Golgi tendon reflex = bisynaptic; Withdrawal reflex = polysynaptic
  • Muscle spindle = receptor for stretch reflex; Golgi tendon organ = receptor for inverse stretch reflex
  • Nuclear bag → dynamic (tendon jerk); Nuclear chain → static (tone)
  • Ia afferent → both bag & chain; II afferent → chain only
  • Gamma motor neuron → intrafusal fibres; Alpha motor neuron → extrafusal fibres

Clinical One-Liners

  • Clasp-knife spasticity → UMN lesion
  • Lead-pipe rigidity → Parkinsonism
  • Hypotonia → LMN lesion or neo-cerebellar syndrome
  • Clonus → definitive sign of UMN lesion
  • Hyperreflexia → UMNL, hyperthyroidism, nervousness
  • Hyporeflexia → LMNL, hypothyroidism, sleep
  • Golgi tendon reflex = protective against rupture (glycine-mediated inhibition)
