Synaptic Transmission & the Neuromuscular Junction

How one neuron talks to the next — and how a nerve talks to muscle. From synaptic anatomy and the calcium-triggered release cascade, to EPSPs, IPSPs, referred pain, and the drugs and diseases that hijack every step.

01 · Foundations

Synapse Anatomy & Structural Classification

A synapse is the point of functional contact between two excitable cells. Between two neurons it is called a neuronal synapse; between a neuron and a muscle fibre it is a neuromuscular junction. Every synapse has a presynaptic and a postsynaptic side.

Presynaptic elementAlways the axon

The presynaptic side of a neuronal synapse is always the axon terminal, known by several interchangeable names:

  • Axon terminal / synaptic terminal
  • Synaptic knob or presynaptic knob
  • Terminal bouton ("bouton" = button — the ending is shaped like a small button)

Postsynaptic elementDendrite, soma, or axon

The postsynaptic membrane carries the neurotransmitter receptors. Depending on which part of the receiving neuron is contacted, the synapse is named structurally as below.

TypeContactFrequency
AxodendriticPresynaptic axon → postsynaptic dendritesMost common
AxosomaticPresynaptic axon → postsynaptic soma / cell bodyCommon
AxoaxonalPresynaptic axon → postsynaptic axonLeast common
02 · Foundations

Physiological Types: Electrical vs Chemical Synapse

Functionally, every synapse is either electrical or chemical — and the two differ in mechanism, speed, and how common they are.

Electrical synapseDirect ion flow

  • Ions (e.g. Na⁺) pass directly from presynaptic to postsynaptic cytoplasm through a gap junction.
  • Na⁺ influx depolarizes the presynaptic cell and crosses straight into the postsynaptic cell.
  • ⭐⭐ Very rare and ⭐⭐ very fast — the two defining features.
  • Location: retina of the eye and the olfactory bulb (part of the olfactory pathway).

Chemical synapseNeurotransmitter-mediated

  • ⭐⭐⭐ Most common type of synapse in the nervous system.
  • Transmission occurs through neurotransmitters — chemical messengers released by the presynaptic terminal.
  • The impulse cannot cross without a neurotransmitter: release → diffusion across the cleft → receptor binding → local potential → summation → action potential.
  • This is the type covered in full mechanistic detail in the next section.
03 · Chemical Transmission

The Chemical Transmission Cascade

⭐⭐⭐ The single most tested sequence in this lecture. Learn it as one continuous chain — every downstream step depends on the one before it.

1

Impulse reaches the synaptic knob

The action potential travels down the axon and arrives at the presynaptic terminal (synaptic knob).

2

Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open

Named "voltage-gated" because the arriving action potential (a voltage change) is what opens them.

3

Calcium influx into the presynaptic knob

Ca²⁺ enters the terminal and binds an intracellular calcium-binding protein (the same mechanism used for acetylcholine release and, elsewhere, for insulin release from pancreatic β-cells).

4

Ca²⁺–protein complex activates an enzyme

E.g. a calcium–calmodulin dependent enzyme. This enzyme is proposed to lyse the fine cytoskeletal meshwork that normally tethers vesicles away from the membrane.

5

Vesicles move to the membrane

Once released from the meshwork, synaptic vesicles travel toward the presynaptic membrane.

6

Contact → fusion → breakdown

Vesicles contact the membrane, fuse with it, and the fusion zone then breaks down — this whole process is exocytosis.

7

Neurotransmitter release into the synaptic cleft

The synaptic cleft is the fluid-filled gap between pre- and postsynaptic membranes.

8

Diffusion & receptor binding

Neurotransmitter diffuses across the cleft and binds a specific receptor on the postsynaptic membrane, which determines whether the effect is excitatory or inhibitory.

9

Channel opening → local potential → summation

Receptor binding opens Na⁺/Ca²⁺ (excitatory) or Cl⁻/K⁺ (inhibitory) channels, generating a local potential that undergoes spatial summation across many occupied receptors to reach threshold.

04 · Chemical Transmission

Excitatory & Inhibitory Neurotransmitters

Whether a neurotransmitter excites or inhibits depends entirely on which channel its receptor opens.

Excitatory · opens Na⁺ / Ca²⁺ channelsEPSP → summation → action potential

  • Acetylcholine (ACh)
  • Glutamate — ⭐⭐⭐ the most important excitatory neurotransmitter in the brain

Depolarization from Na⁺/Ca²⁺ entry produces an excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP). Because hundreds of receptors are occupied simultaneously across the postsynaptic membrane, their local potentials undergo spatial summation ("spatial" = summation in space) to reach an action potential.

Inhibitory · opens Cl⁻ / K⁺ channelsIPSP → moves away from threshold

  • GABA — ⭐⭐⭐ "the" main inhibitory neurotransmitter (pharmacology never says "a" GABA — it's always referred to as the principal one)
  • Glycine

Cl⁻ influx or K⁺ efflux both hyperpolarize the membrane, producing an inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP). This pushes the membrane potential further from threshold (e.g. from −70 mV toward −100 mV), slowing or blocking conduction.

🩺 Clinical correlation — Hepatic encephalopathy

Liver failure impairs ammonia detoxification (no urea cycle in the brain). The brain instead disposes of ammonia by combining it with α-ketoglutarate → glutamate → glutamine. This conversion depletes brain glutamate stores, causing inhibition of synaptic transmission — confusion, coma, and potentially death. The root problem (liver failure, ↑ammonia) is systemic, but the manifestations appear in the brain, hence "hepatic encephalopathy."

🩺 Clinical correlation — Alcohol, GABA & memory blackouts

Alcohol potentiates GABA activity, including in the hippocampus. This is why heavily intoxicated people can talk extensively yet remember nothing the next day — GABA activity prevents the synapses in the hippocampus from building new memories. The same GABA pathway is exploited therapeutically by phenobarbital (epilepsy), general anaesthetics, sedatives, and benzodiazepines such as diazepam (Valium), which works by inhibiting GABA catabolism and helping GABA open chloride channels.

05 · Synaptic Properties

Properties of Synaptic Transmission

Eight properties define how information actually moves through chains of synapses — several are classic clinical-correlation questions.

1 · One-way conduction

An axon can conduct an impulse in either direction, but a chemical synapse cannot: only the presynaptic side has neurotransmitter, and only the postsynaptic side has receptors — so conduction is forced one way, from neurotransmitter source to receptor.

2 · Synaptic delay

The time taken for an impulse to cross one synapse — from Ca²⁺-channel opening through to postsynaptic channel opening — is 0.5–0.9 ms. That the nervous system calls half a millisecond a "delay" says something about how fast it really is.

3 · Central delay

Central delay = synaptic delay × number of synapses in the pathway

If a pathway contains 3 synapses, multiply the per-synapse delay by 3 to get the total central delay along that route.

4 · Synaptic fatigue

Rapidly repeated nerve impulses deplete neurotransmitter stores at the presynaptic terminal, so the synapse temporarily fails to transmit — it "tires."

🩺 Clinical correlation — Epilepsy

Patients with epilepsy are advised to avoid heavy sensory/motor stimulation (flashing lights, screens, exhaustion) because rapid repeated impulses can precipitate a seizure. During a seizure itself, the same rapid repeated firing eventually depletes neurotransmitter stores — this fatigue is protective: it relaxes the skeletal muscle after the fit, preventing sustained tetanic contraction that would otherwise exhaust the patient's energy stores.

5 · Convergence — and referred pain

Convergence: axon terminals from several different presynaptic neurons all synapse onto one postsynaptic neuron.

🩺 Clinical correlation — Referred pain

The diaphragm develops embryologically in the neck, so its nerve supply (phrenic nerve) descends from cervical segments C3–C5. Skin afferents from the shoulder enter the spinal cord at the same cervical level. Diaphragmatic pain fibres and shoulder-skin pain fibres converge onto the same second-order neuron, which ascends to the brain — the brain, used to interpreting that neuron's signal as "shoulder skin," misattributes diaphragmatic pain to the shoulder. The same convergence logic explains cardiac pain referred to the inner arm and appendicular pain referred to the umbilicus.

6 · Divergence — and pain relief by touch

Divergence: one presynaptic axon branches to synapse on many postsynaptic neurons (e.g. one fibre branching to five, or five to ten).

🩺 Clinical correlation — Why rubbing an injury eases pain

Two mechanisms, both from the touch pathway: (1) touch fibres conduct faster than pain fibres, so the "rubbing" sensation arrives first; (2) the touch fibre diverges — one branch continues as the touch pathway, and a collateral branch synapses on the pain pathway, releasing an inhibitory neurotransmitter that blocks the pain fibre from exciting its second-order neuron in the spinal cord. This is the physiological basis of "gating" pain with touch.

7 · Habituation

A gradual decline in response to repeated impulses that are irrelevant to the person — e.g. tuning out background chatter while walking through a crowded market.

8 · Sensitization

Sensitization = potentiation of a synapse that has become habituated, restoring its responsiveness. It matters when habituation happens to something important — e.g. losing track of what you're reading. Sensitization is achieved by pairing the original stimulus with a new, stronger stimulus: changing position, moving the neck, pinching yourself, standing up and walking, or any activity that "re-primes" the synapse. This is why rote memorization (e.g. reciting texts) is often paired with rhythmic movement.

07 · Neuromuscular Junction

NMJ & Excitation–Contraction Coupling

The neuromuscular junction is the synapse between a motor nerve and skeletal muscle, at a specialised postsynaptic zone called the motor end plate.

Transmission at the NMJAlways acetylcholine, always excitatory

  • Release follows the same mechanism as the neuronal chemical synapse (Ca²⁺ influx → vesicle fusion → exocytosis).
  • The neurotransmitter is always acetylcholine — never a question of excitatory vs inhibitory at the NMJ, it is always excitatory.
  • ACh diffuses across the cleft and binds nicotinic receptors on the motor end plate.
  • This generates a motor end-plate potential, which — like an EPSP — summates into a full muscle action potential. A single release event can occupy roughly 1000 receptors and reliably trigger an action potential.

Excitation–contraction couplingFrom surface AP to filament sliding

  1. Action potential spreads inward along T-tubules (transverse tubules).
  2. T-tubules abut the terminal cisternae of the sarcoplasmic reticulum — the cell's calcium store.
  3. Ca²⁺ exits the cisternae through the ryanodine receptor channel (blocked pharmacologically by dantrolene in malignant hyperthermia).
  4. Ca²⁺ binds troponin C.
  5. Because Ca²⁺–troponin C binding is stronger than the troponin I–actin interaction, troponin I and tropomyosin are displaced off the actin active site.
  6. Myosin heads bind the exposed actin, producing sliding filament shortening — contraction.
08 · Neuromuscular Junction

Modification of Neuromuscular Transmission

Disease, toxins, and drugs can interrupt neuromuscular transmission at almost every step of the cascade above. This is one of the highest-yield comparison zones in neurophysiology.

Condition / agentTargetMechanismEffect
Myasthenia gravis ⭐⭐⭐ Muscle-type nicotinic ACh receptor Autoimmune antibodies destroy receptors or cross-link them to each other Fluctuating weakness, worse with use; predominantly proximal muscles (e.g. difficulty combing hair, raising arms)
Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome ⭐⭐⭐ Presynaptic voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels Antibodies block calcium channels at the nerve ending ↓ Calcium influx → ↓ acetylcholine release → muscle weakness
α-Bungarotoxin Nicotinic ACh receptor Component of snake venom; binds the receptor irreversibly (unlike ACh's normal bind-and-release cycle) Blocks neuromuscular transmission → paralysis
Botulinum toxin ⭐⭐ Presynaptic release machinery From anaerobic Clostridium botulinum (classically improperly canned food); prevents ACh release Flaccid paralysis; death from respiratory muscle paralysis
Curare Nicotinic ACh receptor Non-depolarizing blocker — binds reversibly, prevents depolarization Muscle relaxation; historically used by South American indigenous hunters on arrow tips, later adopted as a surgical muscle relaxant
Succinylcholine Nicotinic ACh receptor Depolarizing blocker — resembles ACh, triggers initial depolarization, then sustained depolarization prevents normal cycling Muscle relaxation used during general anaesthesia
Neostigmine / physostigmine Acetylcholinesterase Reversible anticholinesterase — prolongs ACh action at remaining receptors Therapeutic in myasthenia gravis
Organophosphates / nerve gas ⭐⭐⭐ Acetylcholinesterase Irreversible inhibition — ACh accumulates and keeps acting continuously Continuous contraction/spasm of respiratory muscles (diaphragm, intercostals) → respiratory failure and death; used in insecticides and as chemical weapons
✓ Why muscle relaxants are given with general anaesthesia

Muscle relaxants such as curare or succinylcholine let the anaesthesiologist achieve adequate muscle relaxation for surgery using a lower dose of anaesthetic agent — reducing the risks that come with deeper anaesthesia.

🩺 Why acetylcholinesterase matters

Acetylcholine is normally terminated by reuptake or by enzymatic breakdown. The enzyme acetylcholinesterase hydrolyses the ester bond in ACh, splitting it into acetate + choline. Blocking this enzyme — reversibly or irreversibly — is the shared mechanism behind treatments for myasthenia gravis and the lethality of organophosphate poisoning; the difference is duration and control of the block.

09 · Exam Zone

High-Yield Exam Pearls

  • ⭐⭐⭐Chemical transmission cascade: action potential → Ca²⁺ influx → neurotransmitter exocytosis is one of the most highly tested sequences in neurophysiology.
  • ⭐⭐⭐Glutamate is the primary excitatory neurotransmitter in the brain.
  • ⭐⭐⭐GABA is heavily emphasized as the main inhibitory neurotransmitter — always "the," never "a."
  • ⭐⭐Hepatic encephalopathy: liver failure → ↑ ammonia → ammonia consumes brain glutamate (converted to glutamine) → ↓ excitatory transmission → coma.
  • ⭐⭐⭐Myasthenia gravis vs Lambert–Eaton: MG = antibodies against muscle-type nicotinic ACh receptors (proximal weakness); Lambert–Eaton = antibodies against presynaptic voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels.
  • ⭐⭐Botulinum toxin prevents ACh release → flaccid paralysis, often fatal via respiratory paralysis.
  • ⭐⭐⭐Organophosphate poisoning: irreversible acetylcholinesterase inhibition → continuous ACh action → continuous contraction → death by respiratory failure.
10 · Exam Zone

Clinical Pearls

Referred pain & convergence

Diaphragmatic pain felt in the shoulder (C3–C5 dermatomes) is the classic example of neural convergence, where different nerve fibres synapse on the same postsynaptic neuron in the spinal cord.

Pain modulation by touch (divergence)

Rubbing an injured area reduces pain because touch fibres are faster than pain fibres and send inhibitory collaterals (divergence) that block pain transmission at the spinal cord.

Epilepsy & synaptic fatigue

Avoiding flashing lights and heavy stimulation limits rapid repeated impulses; during a seizure, the same repeated firing depletes neurotransmitter stores, protectively relaxing muscle and preventing fatal tetanic contraction.

Alcoholism & GABA

Alcohol targets GABA receptors, particularly in the hippocampus, preventing memory consolidation during intoxication (blackouts). Diazepam works similarly by helping GABA open chloride channels.

Studying & sensitization

When habituation sets in while studying, a new stronger stimulus — moving around, shifting posture — potentiates the synapse (sensitization) and restores focus.

Muscle relaxants in surgery

Curare (non-depolarizing) and succinylcholine (depolarizing) paralyze muscle, letting anaesthesiologists use a lower, safer dose of general anaesthetic.

11 · Exam Zone

Difficult Medical Vocabulary

Bouton / terminal bouton
French for "button" — the button-shaped swelling at the end of an axon terminal where neurotransmitter is stored and released.
Synaptic cleft
The fluid-filled gap separating the presynaptic and postsynaptic membranes, crossed by neurotransmitter via diffusion.
Exocytosis
Vesicle contact, fusion with, and breakdown at the plasma membrane, releasing vesicle contents to the outside of the cell.
Spatial summation
Addition of many simultaneous local potentials arising at different receptor sites across the membrane, together reaching threshold.
Depolarization / hyperpolarization
Movement of membrane potential toward threshold (less negative) or away from it (more negative), respectively.
Voltage-gated channel
An ion channel that opens in response to a change in membrane voltage, as opposed to ligand- or mechanically-gated channels.
Calmodulin
An intracellular calcium-binding protein that, once bound to Ca²⁺, activates downstream enzymes (calcium–calmodulin dependent enzymes).
Motor end plate
The specialised postsynaptic region of a skeletal muscle fibre at the neuromuscular junction, rich in nicotinic ACh receptors.
T-tubule (transverse tubule)
An invagination of the muscle cell membrane that carries the action potential deep into the fibre, adjacent to the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
Ryanodine receptor channel
The calcium-release channel of the sarcoplasmic reticulum; pharmacologically blocked by dantrolene in malignant hyperthermia.
Tetany
Sustained, continuous muscle contraction without relaxation, as opposed to a normal contraction–relaxation cycle.
Flaccid vs spastic paralysis
Flaccid = muscle stuck in relaxation (e.g. botulinum toxin); spastic = muscle stuck in contraction (e.g. organophosphate poisoning).
Habituation vs sensitization
Habituation = declining response to a repeated, irrelevant stimulus; sensitization = restoring/potentiating that response with a novel, stronger stimulus.
